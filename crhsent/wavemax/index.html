What was lost

16 months of growth, erased overnight

This is the real Google Search Console record for WaveMAX Austin: sixteen months of patient, compounding local-search growth — three rounds of careful SEO and local advertising that lifted daily impressions from a standing start to a peak of 1,427/day. Then, on May 1, 2026, the site was handed to a new marketing vendor's platform. Within a week, daily impressions collapsed from ~1,000 to under 30 — not a dip, a wipeout. Sixteen months of work, gone overnight, given to a vendor that bills itself as "fast, secure, found on Google" and — as the receipts that follow show — delivers little of it. The store did not begin to reappear in Google until August 5, 2026 — more than three months later, and only after repeated written demands — and even then only at roughly half its former rate.

What the numbers show — in plain terms

In the store's own Google Search Console, WaveMAX Austin earned 28,882 impressions in April 2026, the month before the switch. The next full month, June, it earned 79. July: 6. That is not a slow decline — it is a page disappearing from Google. The cause was a change the platform made to how the page tells Google its own web address (its "canonical" address); the platform's own code even carries a developer comment acknowledging it was "causing Google to deindex" the Austin page.

Search visibility is earned slowly and lost quickly. When the corrected address finally took hold on August 5, 2026 — more than three months later, and only after repeated written demands — the page began ranking again, but at about 704 impressions a day, roughly half the ~1,400/day it had reached before. Three months of a local store's search presence were simply lost, and the recovery is only partial.

Why the heavy, slow pages make this worse: Google's crawler does not wait for slow pages. When a page is 5–6 MB and takes 15–20 seconds to display, Google reads fewer of the store's pages, less often, and files many of them under "crawled — currently not indexed." A bloated, slow platform does not just frustrate customers — it directly limits how much of the store Google will show at all.

Why the gap — and why it matters to a local business

Speed isn't vanity. Google treats page speed as a ranking factor, and every extra second of load time measurably costs a local store walk-in conversions on mobile. The current platform is a heavy WordPress build that ships a 400 KB+ page and dozens of scripts before a visitor sees anything. A lean, pre-built page on a global CDN loads in under a second; the platform does not — and the lost customers and lost rankings land on the local operator, not the vendor.

What these gaps mean for a local business

These aren't checkbox formalities. Missing security controls make a location's customer data easier to steal, the site easier to hijack or impersonate, and — when something goes wrong — the liability usually lands on the local operator, not the platform. Customers hand over their trust the moment they book. Trust takes years to earn and seconds to lose — and on the current platform these gaps are network-wide.

A word on review-gating — a real liability

"Review funnel" widgets that route happy customers to public review sites while diverting unhappy ones to a private form are review-gating. That practice violates Google's terms and is reachable under the FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on Consumer Reviews — civil penalties run to roughly $53,000 per violation. If a review control is added to a location's site without proper diligence, the liability lands on the operator. A review control must funnel compliantly and never gate; a control added without diligence puts that penalty exposure on the operator.

This already happened. WaveMAX's current marketing partner published a review-gating control on every franchise page and left it live for weeks. It took a franchisee flagging the liability to get it pulled from the Austin page — and multiple more warnings before it came off the rest. Every operator wore that exposure the entire time.

Built by trial and error, not engineering — and it shows

A well-built web page loads each tool it needs once, in a deliberate order. The franchisor's franchise-recruitment page instead loads the same tool — a library called jQuery — three separate times (two different versions plus a compatibility patch), and bolts a speed-optimization trick on top that installs a fake placeholder in the real library's place so the real copies can load later. The placeholder is a stand-in only: it has none of the library's actual functions.

The result is a page that breaks in ways whoever assembled it plainly does not understand. A script partway down the page tries to wire up the "Request Information" form by calling a standard jQuery function, .on() — but at that moment the shortcut it uses ($) points at the placeholder, not the real library, and the placeholder has no such function. So it crashes every single time the page opens — the browser reports $(...).on is not a function. The most visible symptom for a real visitor: on the "Become a Franchisee" page, the navigation menu opens and then will not close, because the code that should close it died in the same crash. The lead form's submit handler never attaches either.

This is the signature of automated optimizations and hand-written code layered on top of each other without anyone testing the result — what has come to be called "vibe coding." Anyone maintaining the site would see the red error in a browser's developer console every time they loaded the page; it has simply never been fixed. Why this matters — and that a proper alternative already exists

If a laundromat's website goes dark on a Saturday, it loses the bookings and the walk-ins that came with it. A single shared-hosting box has no backup — if it hiccups, the site is simply down. This is not hypothetical: a compliant, high-availability alternative already exists and runs in production today. The reference deployment runs active-active in two independent Phoenix data centers behind a load balancer — both serve traffic at once, so if one fails the other carries all of it automatically, with nobody on call. That is the difference between "a website" and infrastructure a business can actually depend on — and it is available now.
